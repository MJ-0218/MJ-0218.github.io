Hello hello!
Year-end sales are coming, and scent gift sets are always a hit.
Before you add one to cart, here's how the US FDA looks at the words printed on the box.

![Scent label card](/assets/cards/my-perfume-aromatherapy-label-claims-summary.png)

## Perfume is a cosmetic
If something goes on your body to make you more attractive, it counts as a cosmetic under US law.
Perfume, cologne and aftershave all fall into that group.

## Some claims change the category
Things get interesting when the label promises an effect on your body.
FDA lists examples that make a scented product a **drug**:

- Easing muscle aches
- Soothing headaches
- Helping people sleep
- Treating colic

So a pillow spray promising better sleep is making a much bigger claim than a nice smell.

## "Essential oil" isn't a legal term
There's no regulatory definition for "essential oils", FDA says.
And plant ingredients are treated the same as ingredients from any other source.

A massage oil meant to smooth skin is a cosmetic.
Claim it relaxes muscles, and it becomes a drug, or possibly both.

## Room sprays are a different shelf
Scented things that don't go on your body, like air fresheners, dryer sheets and fabric softener, are handled by the US Consumer Product Safety Commission instead.

## Easy checks before buying
- Treat "helps you sleep" or "relieves pain" on a scent product as a big claim, not a bonus.
- If you're sensitive to scents, look for fragrance-free versions of everyday items.
- Gifting? Pick a scent the person already likes.
