Hello hello!
Found a half-used bottle of sunscreen at the back of the drawer?
Before you slap it on for the weekend, let's check if it's still doing its job.

The US FDA answers this one clearly, so here's the short version.

![Sunscreen expiry card](/assets/cards/my-does-sunscreen-expire-summary.png)

## Yes, sunscreen expires

FDA requires nonprescription drugs, sunscreen included, to carry an expiry date.
The exception is when the maker's stability testing shows the product stays stable for **at least three years**.

## What if there's no date on it?

FDA says a sunscreen without an expiry date should be treated as **expired three years after purchase**.
So if you can't remember when you bought it, that's your answer.

FDA's simple rules:

- Past the expiry date? Don't use it.
- No expiry date and bought more than three years ago? Don't use it.
- No idea how old it is? Throw it out.

Expired sunscreen may not be safe or effective anymore, FDA explains.

## Heat shortens the story

Leaving the bottle in a hot car or in direct sun isn't good for it.
FDA suggests wrapping containers in a towel or keeping them in the shade when you're outdoors for long.

In Malaysian weather, a car dashboard is the worst place for it, lah.

## A simple habit

Write the month you opened it on the bottle with a marker.
And if you use enough, a bottle shouldn't last that long anyway.

FDA says an average adult needs about one ounce, roughly a shot glass, to cover the whole body.

## Buying a new one?

Check the expiry date on the box before you pay, especially during big sales.
Look for **broad spectrum** with **SPF 15 or higher**, as FDA recommends, in a texture you'll actually reapply.
