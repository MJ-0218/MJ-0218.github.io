Ever wondered if the price you see online is the same one your neighbor sees?
That's exactly the question the **Federal Trade Commission** is now asking some of the biggest brands in America.

The FTC has ordered **nine companies** to hand over information on how they use personal data to set prices, and how gig workers get paid.

![FTC surveillance pricing card](/assets/cards/us-ftc-surveillance-pricing-probe-9-companies-2026-summary.png)

## Which companies are on the list
Here are the nine companies named in the inquiry:

- **Airlines:** Delta, United, JetBlue
- **Ride-share and delivery:** Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart
- **Retail:** Walmart, Target

If you fly, order a ride, get groceries delivered or shop at a big-box store, odds are you use at least one of them.

## What the FTC wants to know
Its goal is to see whether the price of a ticket, a ride, a food delivery or another product **changes from one person to the next**.

Factors it's looking at include **loyalty program participation** and the **analysis of personal data** by algorithms.
FTC Chairman **Andrew Ferguson** said the agency will "scrutinize the opaque methodology" companies may be using to decide what consumers pay, or what gig workers earn, for the same product or service.

## It's about drivers' pay, too
For ride-share and delivery apps, the FTC is also examining **how worker pay is set**.

Offers drivers receive may depend on opaque formulas.
Those could involve data like a driver's **phone usage** or their **history of accepting offers**.

## What "surveillance pricing" means
It's the practice of charging different people different prices based on what a company knows about them, like buying history or online activity.

In January 2025, the FTC said details such as a person's **precise location or browser history** can be used to target individual shoppers with different prices for the same goods.

## States are moving too
Several states have introduced bills to **ban surveillance pricing** and **electronic shelf labels** that could be used to change prices based on shoppers' habits.

## Will anything change right away?
Not yet.
This is an information-gathering inquiry, not a fine or a new rule.

It's also unclear whether it will lead to new rules.
There's a limit, too: the FTC's "unfair or deceptive" authority excludes banking and airlines, so it can't fine or regulate the carriers named.

## A few habits worth keeping
- Before a big purchase, **compare the price** while signed out or in a private browser window.
- Read the **terms of loyalty programs** so you know what data you're sharing.
- **Screenshot prices** you're offered, in case you want to compare later.
